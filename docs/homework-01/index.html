02 How it works

For y² = x³ + ax + b, take a = 0, b = 7, p = 17. Coordinates are residues 0–16 in F17; arithmetic is modulo 17. G = (1, 5) lies on the curve: 5² ≡ 1³ + 7 ≡ 8.

For P = (x₁, y₁) and R = (x₂, y₂), addition gives P + R = (x₃, y₃):

Distinct x-values: λ = (y₂ − y₁)(x₂ − x₁)−1

Doubling, y₁ ≠ 0: λ = (3x₁² + a)(2y₁)−1

x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂
y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁

Every operation is modulo p.

Field division multiplies by a modular inverse: dd−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Extended Euclid finds ud + vp = 1, so d−1 = u mod p. Ordinary division is not valid here.

O is the identity: P + O = P. Negation gives −P = (x, −y mod p), and P + (−P) = O. Doubling with y = 0 also gives O, avoiding division by zero (RFC 6090, §§2–3).

Worked example: G + G

For G = (1, 5), the denominator is 2 × 5 = 10. Its inverse is 12 because 10 × 12 ≡ 1 (mod 17). Thus λ ≡ 3 × 12 ≡ 2, x₃ ≡ 2² − 2 ≡ 2, and y₃ ≡ 2(1 − 2) − 5 ≡ 10. Hence 2G = (2, 10).

Starting at O, j additions of G give jG. The order n is the smallest positive integer with nG = O. Private scalars use 1 ≤ k < n; zero and multiples of n give O.

03 Statistical exploration

Every valid k, from 1 to n − 1, produces a different nonidentity subgroup point. Equal weight on these scalars gives uniform points, but not necessarily uniform coordinates in F17.

The default subgroup contains 9 of the full curve’s 18 elements, including O.

Coordinate frequencies

Inverse points (x, y) and (x, −y mod 17) share x. For default G = (1, 5), x = 1, 2, 5, 12 each occurs twice; eight distinct y-values occur once.

Input–output relationships

Pearson’s r measures linear association between k and each coordinate:

r(k, y)

Low correlation implies neither independence nor security: k determines Q. The value depends on the generator and numeric encoding; it is undefined with too few observations or zero variance.

Neighboring scalars and changed bits

Hamming distance counts differing bits in x || y between kG and (k + 1)G. Both scalars remain valid, excluding O and wraparound.

Adding one can flip several input bits. These changes illustrate avalanche-like behavior, rather than testing the classical single-input-bit avalanche criterion used to study hashes.

Changing the base point

The base (2, 10) = 2(1, 5) generates the same order-9 subgroup because 2 and 9 are coprime. It reorders points, preserving coordinate and bit frequencies but changing scalar relationships. Bases (5, 9), (3, 0), and (6, 6) have orders 3, 2, and 18, giving different datasets.

These exhaustive results describe the selected subgroup and encoding; they do not establish cryptographic randomness or security.

Conclusions

The default result is 6(1, 5) = (5, 8). This base has order 9: 9G = O and (k + 9)G = kG. The patterns reflect its subgroup. Recovering a scalar here takes at most 17 trials; real ECC relies on hard discrete logarithms in suitable large groups (RFC 6090, §10.1).

Scalar multiplication and hashing are both deterministic, but they have different algebra and security goals:

Scalar multiplication compared with cryptographic hashing
AspectScalar multiplicationCryptographic hash
Input → outputScalar modulo n → group pointMessage → fixed-length digest
Structure(k + ℓ)G = kG + ℓG; periodic modulo nNo corresponding group law
Security goalHard discrete logarithms; used in public-key schemes and signaturesPreimage, second-preimage, and collision resistance

Repeating a point for k and k + n is expected periodicity, not a hash collision claim.
