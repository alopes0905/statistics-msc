02 / Mathematical explanation

How it works

The curve is y² = x³ + ax + b over the finite field Fp, with a = 0, b = 7, p = 17. Coordinates are integers 0–16 and all arithmetic is modulo 17. For example, G = (1, 5) lies on the curve because 5² ≡ 1³ + 7 ≡ 8.

For P = (x₁, y₁) and R = (x₂, y₂), addition gives P + R = (x₃, y₃):

Distinct x-values: λ = (y₂ − y₁)(x₂ − x₁)−1

Doubling, y₁ ≠ 0: λ = (3x₁² + a)(2y₁)−1

x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂
y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁

Every operation is modulo p.

Division uses a modular inverse: d−1 satisfies dd−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Extended Euclid finds u, v with ud + vp = 1, making u mod p the inverse. Ordinary division would give incorrect field arithmetic.

O is the identity: P + O = P. Negation gives −P = (x, −y mod p), so P + (−P) = O. Doubling a point with y = 0 also gives O. These cases avoid division by zero. The group law follows RFC 6090, §§2–3.

Worked example: G + G

With G = (1, 5), the doubling denominator is 2 × 5 = 10. Since 10 × 12 ≡ 1 (mod 17), its inverse is 12. Hence λ ≡ 3 × 12 ≡ 2, x₃ ≡ 2² − 2 ≡ 2, and y₃ ≡ 2(1 − 2) − 5 ≡ 10. Therefore 2G = (2, 10).

Repeated addition starts at O and adds G once per step: after j additions, the result is jG. The first positive n with nG = O is the order of G. Private scalars use 1 ≤ k < n; mathematically, 0G and multiples of n give O.

03 / Statistical exploration

Statistical exploration

For each G of order n, the experiment tests every k from 1 to n − 1. Each nonidentity subgroup point appears once. Equal weight on the scalars is uniform over these points, but their coordinates need not be uniform over F17.

The default subgroup contains 9 of the full curve’s 18 elements, including O.

Coordinate frequencies

Inverse points (x, y) and (x, −y mod 17) share x, but different valid scalars still produce different points. For G = (1, 5), x = 1, 2, 5, 12 each occurs twice; y = 1, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 16 each occurs once.

Five-bit coordinates

The encoding x || y joins two unsigned five-bit coordinates, most significant bit first. For example, (1, 5) becomes 00001 00101: ten bits, with a space for readability.

Input–output relationships

The result is deterministic, but the coordinates are not monotonic in k. Pearson’s r measures their linear association with k in the chosen numeric representation:

r(k, x)
r(k, y)

For G = (1, 5), r(k, x) = 0 and r(k, y) ≈ 0.298. Low correlation implies neither independence nor security: k determines Q. A different encoding or generator can change r. It is undefined with insufficient observations or zero coordinate variance.

Neighboring scalars and changed bits

Hamming distance counts differing bits in x || y for kG and (k + 1)G. Both scalars stay within 1,…,n − 1; there is no comparison through O or wraparound.

For G = (1, 5), the distances are 6, 5, 3, 2, 4, 7, 5. Adding one to k can flip several input bits, so this comparison does not test the classical single-input-bit avalanche criterion.

Changing the base point

The base (2, 10) = 2(1, 5) generates the same order-9 subgroup, since 2 and 9 are coprime. It permutes points, preserving coordinate and bit frequencies while changing scalar relationships and neighboring comparisons. Bases (5, 9), (3, 0), and (6, 6) have orders 3, 2, and 18 respectively, giving different datasets.

Since every valid scalar is tested, the results are exact for the selected subgroup. They describe its algebraic structure, not cryptographic randomness or the security of large curves.

04 / Implementation & conclusions

Implementation & conclusions

The calculations use standard JavaScript without libraries. The excerpts below load directly from the mathematical source file.

Point addition and doubling

Point addition handles O and inverse points first, then uses the appropriate slope for ordinary addition or doubling. Both cases share the coordinate formulas.

Repeated addition

Each iteration adds G and stores the next multiple. The final point is kG; zero iterations give O. The displayed sequence comes from these same calculations.

Supporting code: modular inverse

Extended Euclid computes the inverse needed for field division. A gcd other than one means the inverse does not exist.

Supporting code: Pearson correlation

Pearson’s r divides the sum of centered products by the square root of the two centered sums of squares. Insufficient data or zero variance returns null, displayed as an explanation.

Conclusions

The default calculation gives 6(1, 5) = (5, 8). This base has order 9, so 9G = O, 10G = G, and (k + 9)G = kG. Its coordinate patterns reflect subgroup structure. Recovering a scalar on this toy curve needs at most 17 trials; real ECC relies on difficult discrete logarithms in suitable large groups (RFC 6090, §10.1).

Scalar multiplication and hashing are both deterministic, but they have different algebra and security goals:

Input → outputScalar modulo n → group pointMessage → fixed-length digest

Repeating a point for k and k + n is expected periodicity, not a hash collision claim.
