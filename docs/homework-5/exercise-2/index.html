01 Normal CDF and mathematical foundations

The standard normal density is φ(x)=exp(−x²/2)/√(2π), with CDF Φ(x)=∫₋∞ˣ φ(t)dt. Symmetry gives Φ(0)=½ and Φ(−x)=1−Φ(x); Φ′(x)=φ(x)>0, so the exact CDF is strictly increasing and lies between 0 and 1. Numerical values near 1 can coincide after floating-point rounding.

Starting at zero replaces the infinite lower endpoint: Φ(x)=½+∫₀ˣ φ(t)dt. Substituting t=√2u gives ∫₀ˣ φ(t)dt=(1/√π)∫0x/√2 exp(−u²)du. With the NIST definitions:

erf(z)=(2/√π)∫₀ᶻ exp(−t²)dt,
erfc(z)=1−erf(z),
Φ(x)=½[1+erf(x/√2)]
=½ erfc(−x/√2).

These are exact identities. Naming erf or erfc supplies a special-function representation, not an elementary antiderivative or a numerical implementation. The MathWorld normal-distribution account gives the same relationship.

Why no elementary antiderivative exists

This is Liouville’s theorem on integration in finite terms, from differential algebra. Its exponential-integrability criterion would require a rational function r satisfying r′−2xr=1 for exp(−x²). A finite complex pole of r would produce an uncancellable higher-order pole in r′, so r must be a polynomial. Then −2xr has higher degree than r′ and cannot leave the constant 1. No such rational r exists.

This applies the criterion explained by Churchill, §5, Corollary 5.3 and the Gaussian example; it is not a proof of Liouville’s full theorem. The Gaussian has an antiderivative, represented by erf or an infinite series. The theorem rules out a finite elementary expression.

The ETSU lecture notes, pp. 5–6, motivate the series approach. All Gaussian formulas here use exponent −t²/2; nonstandard normal variables would standardize as (x−μ)/σ, with σ the standard deviation.

Central erf series

Termwise integration of exp(−z²) yields the DLMF series:

erf(z)=(2/√π)Σₖ₌₀∞ (−1)ᵏz²ᵏ⁺¹/[k!(2k+1)].

The recurrence advances each term using the previous one, without factorials or repeated large powers. Compensated summation limits roundoff. On |x|≤2, the adaptive method stops when the next omitted erf term bounds truncation by 2×10⁻¹⁷, or fails after 200 terms (hard limit 256). That bound concerns series truncation in exact arithmetic; it does not bound floating-point cancellation.

Main method: compute the small tail directly

For z>0, substitute t=z+u into Q(z)=∫z∞φ(t)dt:

Q(z)=φ(z)∫₀∞ exp(−zu−u²/2)du.

The hybrid uses the central series for |x|≤2. For 2<|x|≤10 it integrates the transformed positive integrand on [0,8] with a generated, cached 32-point Gauss–Legendre rule. It returns Q(|x|) for negative x, and 1−Q(x) for positive x, while retaining the small complementary probability separately.

For u=8+v, the exponent is at most −8z−32−(z+8)v. Integrating that dominating exponential bounds the omitted probability by φ(z)exp(−8z−32)/(z+8). For z≥2 this is below 8×10⁻²⁴. This is a truncation bound only; quadrature and roundoff errors are checked separately.

Nodes are roots of a recursively evaluated Legendre polynomial, refined by bounded Newton iteration; weights follow 2/[(1−r²)P′₃₂(r)²]. The generated rule integrates polynomials through degree 63 in exact arithmetic; it is validated independently in tests. DLMF §3.5(v) supplies the quadrature framework.

04 Interpretation and conclusions

The exact erf identity expresses the Gaussian integral through a special function. Liouville’s theorem explains the absence of an elementary primitive; it neither removes the antiderivative nor evaluates erf numerically. Finite series and quadrature still require truncation control and floating-point analysis.

Central values tolerate the series representation well. Negative tails require direct positive quantities: at x=−8 a valid result is about 6.22×10⁻¹⁶, not zero. A small absolute error alone can conceal complete relative failure there. For large positive x, Φ can round to exactly 1 while Q remains nonzero and accurately representable.

The hybrid targets central absolute accuracy and tail relative accuracy across the supported interval [−10,10]. Independent tests require absolute CDF error ≤3×10⁻¹⁶ at the stored points and relative small-tail error ≤5×10⁻¹⁴. These are tested numerical tolerances, not a formal error theorem for every real input. The omitted-tail bound does not include quadrature or roundoff.

Simpson improves with refinement until roundoff and cancellation dominate. The erf power series converges mathematically for every finite argument, yet alternating large terms can destroy its double-precision usefulness outside the center. The piecewise method uses at most a few dozen central terms or 32 tail-integrand evaluations, plus one density evaluation; matched-accuracy tables show when extra uniform-integration work fails to recover a tiny tail.

This laboratory covers one standard CDF. Its main implementation, reference data and experiment logic remain separate, and every interactive calculation has explicit work limits.
