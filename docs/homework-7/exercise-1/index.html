01 The four modes of convergence

Let X₁, X₂, … be random variables with candidate limit X. The first three modes compare variables on a common probability space. For convergence in distribution, only their laws are needed. Write Fₙ(x) = P(Xₙ ≤ x) and F(x) = P(X ≤ x).

Four meanings of convergence as n tends to infinity
ModeDefinitionWhat it controls
In probabilityFor every ε > 0, P(|Xₙ−X| > ε) → 0.Fixed-size errors become unlikely.
Almost surely (a.s.)P({ω : limₙ Xₙ(ω) = X(ω)}) = 1.Almost every path eventually stays within each tolerance.
Mean square (L²)Xₙ, X ∈ L² and E[(Xₙ−X)²] → 0.Average squared error vanishes.
In distributionFₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point of F.The CDFs approach the limiting CDF.

02 Implications and counterexamples

Almost sure → In probability → In distribution
L² → In probability → In distribution

Almost sure convergence makes the probability of any ε-error somewhere after index n tend to zero, by continuity of probability for decreasing events. For L², Markov’s inequality bounds the ε-error probability by E[(Xₙ−X)²]/ε². Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution by bounding Fₙ(x) between F(x−ε) and F(x+ε) with an error at most P(|Xₙ−X| > ε), then taking ε down to zero at a continuity point. For a constant limit c, the two CDF values at c−ε and c+ε also give the converse.

Almost surely, without L²
For U uniform on [0,1), let Xₙ = √n · 1{U ≤ 1/n}. Each U > 0 eventually lies outside the shrinking event. The probability of a nonzero value is 1/n, while E[Xₙ²] = n · (1/n) = 1. The rare spikes keep the squared error from vanishing.
L², without almost sure convergence
In the typewriter sequence write n = 2ᵏ+j, where k ≥ 0 and 0 ≤ j < 2ᵏ, and use Xₙ = 1{j/2ᵏ ≤ U < (j+1)/2ᵏ}. The interval length gives E[Xₙ²] = 2⁻ᵏ → 0. Every U in [0,1) belongs to exactly one interval in each stage, so each path has infinitely many ones and infinitely many zeros.
In distribution, without convergence in probability to X
For a fair Rademacher variable X with values ±1, take Xₙ = (−1)ⁿX. All Xₙ have the law of X. At odd indices |Xₙ−X| = 2 almost surely, so P(|Xₙ−X| > 1) alternates between 0 and 1.

The first two examples make a.s. and L² convergence incomparable in general. Together they also disprove the two reverse implications from convergence in probability.

03 LLN, CLT and √n scaling

Let Sₙ = Σᵢ Xᵢ and X̄ₙ = Sₙ/n for iid observations with mean μ and finite variance σ². Linearity gives E[X̄ₙ] = μ. Expanding the variance of the sum gives:

Var(Sₙ) = Σᵢ Var(Xᵢ) + 2Σᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ) = nσ².
SD(Sₙ) = σ√n. Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n. SD(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n.

The last equality for Var(Sₙ) uses independence. Pairwise zero covariance also suffices for adding the variances. Dependence can change their growth: if all Xᵢ are the same variable, Var(Sₙ) = n²σ². Some weakly dependent processes retain √n scaling with a long-run variance that includes covariances. With infinite variance the classical variance derivation does not apply.

Weak LLN. Chebyshev gives P(|X̄ₙ−μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0. Thus X̄ₙ converges in probability to μ. This proves the finite-variance version. Strong LLN. For iid observations with E|X₁| < ∞, X̄ₙ → μ almost surely, even without a finite variance.

L² consistency. In the finite-variance case E[(X̄ₙ−μ)²] = σ²/n. For any square-integrable estimator T of θ, expanding (T−E[T]+E[T]−θ)² eliminates the cross term upon taking expectation, giving MSE(T) = Var(T) + Bias(T)². This is risk under squared-error loss. Vanishing MSE implies consistency in probability.

Classical iid CLT. For 0 < σ² < ∞, multiplying the centered mean by √n restores variance σ². Dividing by σ gives variance 1 at every n. The CLT identifies the limiting law of those fluctuations:

Zₙ = √n(X̄ₙ−μ)/σ = (Sₙ−nμ)/(σ√n) →ᵈ N(0,1).

The LLN concerns the unscaled mean approaching μ. The CLT concerns the CDF of Zₙ and makes no assertion of almost sure convergence or convergence in probability of Zₙ to a normal variable. The normal law approximates a sampling distribution, which is distinct from the population law.

For the sample mean the standard error is σ/√n, estimated by s/√n. With the iid assumptions above and a consistent s, this scale supports approximate confidence intervals such as X̄ₙ ± 1.96s/√n and standardized hypothesis tests. Other asymptotically normal estimators may have their own variance formulas. Variance scaling alone does not establish a CLT.

04 Reproducible numerical demonstration

Bernoulli(p) takes values 0 and 1 with μ = p and σ² = p(1−p). Rademacher takes −1 and +1 with equal probability, giving μ = 0 and σ² = 1. Each trajectory supplies a single prefix at each checkpoint.

Checkpoints 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1,000. Limits are 2–2,000 trajectories, ε from 0.001 to 2, and integer seeds from 1 to 2,147,483,646.

JavaScript and Web Workers enable the simulation.

Variances use m−1, and empirical MSE uses m. Wide tables scroll horizontally.

Park–Miller uses fixed blocks of 1,000 draws per trajectory, so increasing m or the maximum n preserves existing prefixes. The stream is deterministic and approximates iid sampling. Chebyshev is a bound capped at 1, and the deviation count compares integer sums with exact decimal thresholds so equality is included. More trajectories reduce Monte Carlo noise in the summaries. The sampling law of one mean depends on n and the chosen model.

Both models have discrete finite-n laws. The density histogram and normal overlay illustrate the CLT comparison, whose quality depends on n, p and binning. Finite paths illustrate the LLN and cannot establish almost sure convergence. The exact variance and MSE formulas explain the decay seen in the simulation.
