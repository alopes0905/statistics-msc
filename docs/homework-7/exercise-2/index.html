01 Conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem

For P(B) > 0, conditioning on B restricts attention to B and rescales its probability to one. Thus P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B). The product rule follows by multiplication. If P(A) > 0 as well, the same joint event has two factorizations:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)P(B) = P(B | A)P(A).
P(A | B) = P(B | A)P(A)/P(B).

The two conditionals use different denominators. For a mutually exclusive, exhaustive set of hypotheses Hᵢ, the intersections E ∩ Hᵢ partition E. Adding their probabilities gives the evidence, then dividing gives the updated probability:

P(E) = Σᵢ P(E | Hᵢ)P(Hᵢ).
P(Hⱼ | E) = P(E | Hⱼ)P(Hⱼ)/P(E), for P(E) > 0.

Zero-prior cases contribute zero joint probability. In a specified model these are probability calculations. In statistics the conditional model often depends on unknown quantities to be estimated or assigned a prior.

02 Prior, likelihood and posterior

Prior
P(H), or density π(θ), describes uncertainty before the current data. Earlier observations, studies, domain expertise or reference models can inform it. Informative priors concentrate on plausible values. Weakly informative priors leave more room for the data. Both involve modeling choices.
Likelihood
L(θ; D) = p(D | θ) holds the observed data D fixed and varies θ. It measures how the model at each θ accounts for those data. It need not integrate to one over θ. For an alert, P(+ | A) is evidence given a hypothesis. The posterior P(A | +) answers the reverse question.
Posterior
The updated probability of a hypothesis, or the updated distribution of θ, is proportional to likelihood times prior. For continuous θ, divide L(θ; D)π(θ) by the evidence ∫L(t; D)π(t)dt when positive and finite. A posterior predictive probability averages the model for new data over this posterior.

After D₁, the posterior can serve as the prior for the next update. In general π(θ | D₁,D₂) ∝ p(D₂ | θ,D₁)π(θ | D₁). Conditional independence given θ permits replacing the first factor with p(D₂ | θ). Correlated evidence requires a model for that dependence.

03 Two numerical examples

Intrusion alerts and base rates

In this hypothetical detector model, A denotes an intrusion and + denotes an alert. With P(A) = 0.02, sensitivity 0.90 and false-positive rate 0.05, the two contributions to P(+) are 0.018 and 0.049. The resulting positive-alert probability is 0.067 and P(A | +) = 0.018/0.067 ≈ 26.87%. The model expects 180 true alerts and 490 false alerts per 10,000 cases. The larger non-intrusion group explains why false alerts can outnumber true ones even with a low false-positive rate.

For no alert, use the complementary likelihoods 0.10 and 0.95 to obtain P(A | −) = 0.002/0.933 ≈ 0.214%. The positive likelihood ratio LR+ = P(+ | A)/P(+ | Aᶜ) is 18. Dividing the two Bayes numerators gives posterior odds = prior odds × LR+ whenever the ratios and product are defined. The likelihood ratio quantifies the change in odds, which still start at the prior odds.

Learning an unknown success probability

Let θ be the success probability of an attempt under a common model. Suppose θ has a Beta(α,β) prior and the n outcomes are independent conditional on θ. Then the success count s has likelihood C(n,s) θs(1−θ)n−s. Multiplying by the prior kernel θα−1(1−θ)β−1 adds the exponents. The binomial coefficient is constant as a function of θ, and normalizing the resulting kernel gives:

θ | data ∼ Beta(α+s, β+n−s).
E[θ | data] = (α+s)/(α+β+n).
Var(Beta(a,b)) = ab / [(a+b)²(a+b+1)].

With Beta(2,8) and 7 successes in 20 trials, Beta(9,21) has mean 0.30, between the prior mean 0.20 and observed proportion 0.35. For another conditionally independent trial at the same θ, P(next success | data) = ∫θ π(θ | data)dθ = 0.30. This prediction averages over parameter uncertainty.

Processing a success adds one to α and processing a failure adds one to β. Seven success updates take Beta(2,8) to Beta(9,8), and thirteen failure updates take it to Beta(9,21). The order leaves the final exponents unchanged under the same conditionally iid Bernoulli model.

Positive shapes up to 1,000,000 and integer counts with 0 ≤ s ≤ n ≤ 10,000. With n = 0 the posterior equals the prior.

04 Statistical interpretation and limitations

The alert example treats prevalence and detector rates as given. In practice they require representative labeled data and can change across populations or over time. Holding the detector fixed isolates the base-rate effect. The Beta example instead learns an unknown θ and makes the prior’s influence visible.

With 7 successes in 20 trials, the posterior means under Beta(1,1), Beta(2,8) and Beta(20,80) are 8/22 ≈ 0.363636, 9/30 = 0.30 and 27/120 = 0.225. The latter two priors share mean 0.20 but have concentrations 10 and 100. The posterior mean weights the prior mean by (α+β)/(α+β+n) and, for n > 0, the observed proportion by n/(α+β+n). For a fixed proper Beta prior under this model, the prior weight tends to zero as n grows.

Bayesian inference represents uncertainty explicitly, combines earlier information with current data, and supports sequential updates and predictive probabilities under the model. With limited data, the prior can substantially affect the conclusion. Comparing plausible priors exposes that sensitivity.

Selection bias, unrepresentative cases, a changing success probability or dependence across attempts can undermine the likelihood. Complex posterior models may require costly computation. The examples make model-based probability and prediction statements. A causal conclusion would require a suitable design and causal assumptions.
